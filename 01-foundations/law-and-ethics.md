# Law and Ethics

Not a lawyer, this isn't legal advice, laws differ by country — you've heard the
disclaimer. Here's the practical map anyway, because investigators who skip this file
eventually become cautionary tales.

## The core distinctions

**Publicly available ≠ free to use however you want.**
A person's address sitting in a public registry doesn't mean publishing it with
"look where this guy lives" attached is lawful or decent. Context and intent change
everything.

**Access vs. use.**
Most legal trouble in this field comes from *how* you got something, not that you
have it:

- Viewing a public webpage: fine everywhere.
- Logging in with your own account and scraping against Terms of Service: contract
  territory, civil risk, platform bans. Gray zone, do it knowingly.
- Bypassing paywalls, authentication, or access controls: this crosses into computer
  misuse law (CFAA in the US, Computer Misuse Act in the UK, equivalents elsewhere).
  Hard line. Don't.

**Data protection law (GDPR and cousins).**
If you process personal data of EU/UK people, GDPR applies even to OSINT — journalism
and crime prevention have exemptions, casual curiosity does not. Practical version:
collect only what the task requires, note why you collected it, delete when done.

## Bright lines (never cross, no exceptions)

- No unauthorized access to accounts, devices, or systems. Ever.
- No impersonation of real people or officials.
- No buying stolen/hacked data from criminal markets.
- No targeting based on protected characteristics.
- No harassment, threats, or intimidation dressed up as investigation.
- No investigating minors beyond what a legitimate safeguarding role requires.

## The gray zones (knowingly, documented)

- **Password reset reveals** — using "forgot password" flows to see masked contact
  info. Widely taught, legally contested, platform TOS violations. If you use these
  techniques, understand you're in the gray and write down why.
- **Breach data** — checking your own email in HaveIBeenPwned: fine. Searching
  breach dumps for a subject's data: legally risky in many jurisdictions and toxic
  evidence in any proceeding. Journalists handle this with counsel; so should you.
- **Sock puppets** — violating platform TOS with fake accounts rarely brings criminal
  risk but can nuke cases relying on the evidence. Courts dislike tainted methods.

## Ethics checklist (run it before every engagement)

1. Who asked for this and what's the lawful purpose?
2. Could I achieve this with less intrusive collection?
3. Would I be comfortable explaining every step to the subject, a judge, or my editor?
4. Am I about to publish something that endangers someone?
5. Have I separated facts from speculation in my conclusions?

That last question is where reputations are made. Plenty of technically correct
investigations have destroyed innocent people because an analyst published a hunch
as a finding. Confidence language exists for a reason — use it.

## When you find something awful

Child abuse material, credible threats, active planning of violence: stop collecting,
preserve minimal evidence, report to the appropriate authority/platform. Don't
investigate it yourself, don't share it, don't sit on it.
